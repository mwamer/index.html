About

In brief

Dr Wesam Amer is a scholar of media, political communication and higher education. His interdisciplinary research examines how communication technologies, political discourse and institutional structures shape power, conflict and knowledge production. His work focuses on two interconnected areas: digital political communication, media and conflict, social media, artificial intelligence and hypermedia warfare; and higher education, scholasticide, academic resilience and the reconstruction of universities, particularly in Gaza. He has held academic and research positions at Cambridge, Harvard, Newcastle and Hamburg, and served as Dean at Gaza University. He holds a PhD in Media Studies from the University of Hamburg.

Academic journey

From Gaza to Cambridge

His academic journey began in Palestine, where he studied English literature and education, before postgraduate studies in the United Kingdom and Germany.

He completed a BA at Al-Azhar University in Gaza in 2001 and, in 2009, an MA with Distinction in applied linguistics at Anglia Ruskin University. His doctoral research at the University of Hamburg then turned his focus to media and discourse, examining how the international press reported the 2008–09 Gaza war.

A Marie Skłodowska-Curie Fellowship at Newcastle University opened a period of research on digital communication and extremist discourse. A Fulbright Fellowship at Harvard followed in 2022, then a guest research position at the Hans-Bredow-Institut in Hamburg, where he continued research on political communication and digital platforms.

Since 2024 he has been based at the University of Cambridge, first as a CARA / IIE-SRF Fellow in the Department of Sociology, and from 2026 as Affiliated Lecturer at Cambridge Digital Humanities, alongside visiting and fellowship roles at Jesus College, the Centre for Islamic Studies and Homerton College.

Academic biography

A scholar between academia and the world

Dr Amer’s work asks how political and institutional power operates, how societies communicate and produce knowledge under conditions of conflict and transformation, and how media and educational institutions respond to profound technological and political change. It draws on media and communication studies, critical discourse analysis, digital media research, political communication and higher education studies.

Media, political communication and digital technologies

A central strand examines the relationship between media, political communication and power: how political actors construct and circulate narratives, develop communication strategies, mobilise audiences and negotiate public meaning across traditional and digital media.

His earlier research concerned media representations, war reporting and political discourse in relation to the Israeli–Palestinian conflict and the wider Middle East. It then moved towards digital political communication: how political and non-state actors use platforms to communicate, mobilise supporters, construct identities and produce strategic narratives. His interests include Islamist movements, strategic communication and digital activism.

More recently he has asked how platforms, algorithms and artificial intelligence shape communication. Rather than treating digital technologies as neutral channels, he investigates how platform infrastructures, algorithmic systems, content moderation and visibility mechanisms influence how political communication is produced, circulated and received.

His current research explores hypermedia warfare and AI-mediated communication in conflict. It informs his developing approach, Techno-Discursive Critical Analysis (TDCA), which connects the critical interpretation of language, narratives and multimodal content with the technological infrastructures through which discourse is produced, distributed and made visible.

Higher education, knowledge production and scholasticide

A second strand concerns higher education and the institutional consequences of war, occupation and political violence. It treats universities not only as infrastructure or providers of education, but as institutions of knowledge production, cultural continuity, professional development and collective futures.

His work on Gaza examines the destruction of educational institutions and its consequences for academic communities, students, researchers and knowledge systems, engaging with the concept of scholasticide. He is developing the concept of de-scholasticide, which shifts attention from documenting destruction to the intellectual, institutional and practical conditions for restoring and transforming higher education: academic communities, institutional capacity, knowledge infrastructures, research environments and educational opportunities.

The research explores institutional resilience, academic continuity, human-capital regeneration, Palestinian leadership in reconstruction, epistemic self-determination and international academic solidarity, and asks how institutions can move beyond emergency recovery towards sustainable, locally grounded models.

Academic leadership, teaching and institutional development

Alongside research he has extensive experience of teaching, academic management and institutional development, including as Dean of the Faculty of Mass Communication and Languages at Gaza University. His teaching and supervision span media and communication studies, digital media, media and society, political communication, visual communication, social media and research methods.

His approach connects critical inquiry with contemporary media practice and encourages students to understand communication within its technological, political and institutional contexts.

An integrated research agenda

Media and political communication and higher education may seem distinct, but they share one concern: how power, technology, conflict and institutional structures shape the conditions under which societies communicate, produce knowledge and organise their futures.

In media, his research examines how political actors communicate, how digital infrastructures shape public discourse, and how technological systems influence visibility, influence and power. In education, it examines how knowledge institutions are affected by conflict, how academic communities maintain intellectual and professional continuity, and how higher education can be rebuilt to restore institutional capacity and strengthen epistemic autonomy.

Together, these areas form an integrated academic and professional agenda connecting communication, political power, technology, education and knowledge transformation.
